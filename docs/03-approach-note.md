# Approach Note — From Recovered-Stage Telemetry to a Requalification Verdict

*ETL-Avionics · 2026-09-15 · companion to `00-project-plan.md` (plan) and `02-domain-primer.md` (background)*

**What this note covers:** the decision the system has to make, the physics it has to stand on, the surrogate dataset standing in for data that does not exist, and the two candidate formulations for turning a remaining-life estimate into a verdict. It is a design argument, not a results report.

---

## 1. The decision

A recovered avionics unit comes back from a flight. The system must place it in one of three classes:

| Verdict | Meaning | Cost when wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Relaunch** | Cleared for the next mission | Unit fails in flight → **payload and mission lost** |
| **Human review** | Conditional clearance, needs engineer sign-off | Inspection time, schedule slip |
| **Reject / scrap** | Retired | Unnecessary replacement cost |

The two error costs are not symmetric and not close. Scrapping a good unit costs one unit. Clearing a bad one costs a mission. **Every modelling choice downstream is subordinate to that asymmetry** — it is why the target is a distribution rather than a number, and why the metric is not RMSE.

## 3. What is being measured

**Avionics** = the flight electronics: flight computers, navigation sensors (gyros, accelerometers), telemetry transmitters, actuator control.

**Telemetry** = measurement data sent from vehicle to ground during flight. Two classes, and they need different validation:

| Class | Examples | Validation |
|---|---|---|
| Discrete | BIT pass/fail bits, mode flags, reset counters, checksums | **Pattern checking** — is this a legal state, and a legal transition from the last one? |
| Continuous | Vibration, acoustics, rail voltages, temperatures, current draw | **Bound checking** — is the value, and its spectrum, inside the qualified envelope? |

---

## 4. How the electronics actually degrade

| Mechanism | Driver | Standard model | Degradation shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vibration fatigue | Resonant excitation cracks solder joints | Steinberg / PSD-based fatigue | Gradual |
| Thermal cycling | Differential expansion on heat-up / cool-down | Coffin–Manson | Gradual |
| Thermal aging | Temperature-accelerated chemical degradation | Arrhenius | Gradual |
| Total ionizing dose (TID) | Accumulated dose shifts transistor thresholds | Cumulative dose | Gradual |
| Single-event upset (SEU) | One particle flips one bit | Probabilistic | **Abrupt** |
| Mechanical shock | Landing / splashdown event | Shock response spectrum | **Abrupt** |

The split in the last column is the honest boundary of this project. The first four accumulate monotonically and are what a remaining-life model can see coming. The last two are point events; the pipeline must *detect* them (pattern checks, shock bound checks) rather than predict them.

## 5. The surrogate: C-MAPSS

No run-to-failure dataset exists for recovered avionics boards. The available data is NASA **C-MAPSS** (Commercial Modular Aero Propulsion System Simulation) — a MATLAB/Simulink model of a large turbofan, run to failure with injected faults and superimposed sensor noise. It is simulator output, not flight data.

**Generation process, which is what makes it transferable:**

| Element | C-MAPSS | Avionics analogue |
|---|---|---|
| Each unit starts with unknown random initial wear | Manufacturing variation | Same — no unit's history is fully known at recovery |
| Fault injected, grows by exponential wear $h(t) = 1 - d - e^{a t^{b}}$ | Flat, then accelerating | Cumulative damage (Coffin–Manson, Arrhenius) has the same shape |
| Health $H(t) = \min$ over operability margins, failure at $H = 0$ | Fan / HPC / HPT / EGT margins | Requalification margin = min over thermal, timing, power, signal-integrity margins |
| Operating regime (altitude, Mach, throttle) shifts readings independently of health | 6 conditions in FD002/FD004 | Flight phase: ascent, coast, re-entry, landing |
| Asymmetric scoring, late predictions penalised harder | PHM'08 score | The §1 cost asymmetry |

The `min`-over-margins health index is the single most transferable idea. Full mapping table and its limitations: `00-project-plan.md` §3.

**Reading the sensor names.** Digits are gas-path stations — 2 = fan inlet, 24 = LPC outlet, 30 = HPC outlet, 50 = LPT outlet. `Nf` / `Nc` are fan and core spool speeds, `BPR` the bypass ratio, `EGT` exhaust gas temperature (the real-engine life limiter). Readings are altitude-corrected so units are comparable across regimes.

## 6. Why RUL prediction is hard

| Difficulty | Consequence | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Unknown initial wear | Same sensor values, different true remaining life | Model per-unit deviation from a fleet baseline, not absolute level |
| Sensor noise | Single-cycle readings are unreliable | Rolling statistics over a window; never a single cycle |
| Regime changes readings independently of health | Apparent degradation that is only a throttle change | Cluster the operating settings, normalise within regime |
| **No degradation early in life** | A unit with 300 cycles left looks identical to one with 100 | **Cap the RUL target** — piecewise-linear, standard value 125 cycles |

The cap matters more than any feature choice: without it the model is asked to explain a difference that is not present in the data and learns noise instead. Measured on FD001, capping the target cut RMSE from 32.9 to 18.3 and the asymmetric score by 34× (`01-pi-meeting-brief.md` §4).

> **Leakage rule:** no timestamp-derived feature — cycle index, time since start, sequence position — enters the feature set. Cycle number correlates with the label by construction, so any such feature lets the model read the answer instead of the degradation.

## 7. Cost and evaluation

Let $d = \hat{r} - r$ (predicted RUL minus true RUL). $d > 0$ means the unit is believed healthier than it is — the dangerous direction — and must cost more:

$$s = \sum_i \begin{cases} e^{-d_i/13} - 1 & d_i < 0 \ \text{(early, conservative)} \\ e^{\ d_i/10} - 1 & d_i \ge 0 \ \text{(late, unsafe)} \end{cases}$$

Model selection uses this score, not RMSE; RMSE is reported alongside for comparability with published benchmarks, and correlation alongside both because an aggregate score cannot distinguish consistent performance from mixed performance.

*Convention note:* the source paper tabulates $a_1 = 10, a_2 = 13$ while stating late errors are penalised harder. This repo uses the community-standard reading — 13 early, 10 late — documented in `src/metrics.py`.

## 8. Turning a prediction into a decision

This is the part that is a design choice rather than a standard recipe. Two formulations were considered.

### 8.1 Option A — POMDP with RUL as the belief state

| Component | Instantiation |
|---|---|
| Hidden state | True degradation: wear level, fault mode, accumulated damage |
| Observation | Telemetry, vibration, thermal channels |
| Action | Relaunch · inspect · scrap |
| Belief | Posterior over RUL: $P(\text{failure within } x \text{ cycles})$, uncertainty, fault indicators |
| Reward | Negative of the §1 cost structure |

Attractive because inspection costs time, waiting for telemetry delays missions, and scrapping trades a known cost against an uncertain one — a genuinely sequential problem.

**Why it is not the plan.** Learning a policy needs an environment: counterfactual rollouts of what happens *after* an action. C-MAPSS is a fixed batch of run-to-failure trajectories with no actions taken in them and no way to ask what a different decision would have produced. Building an online simulator faithful enough to train against would be a larger project than this one, and its policy would be evidence about the simulator, not about hardware.

### 8.2 Option B — Bayes-risk minimisation *(the proposed approach)*

Requalification between missions is a **one-shot decision on a completed flight record**, not an interactive control problem. That collapses the POMDP into a classical Bayesian decision:

1. Estimate the full conditional distribution $p(r \mid x)$ — quantile regression forest, or a deep model with a quantile / heteroscedastic head. Not a point estimate.
2. Fix a cost matrix $C(a, \cdot)$ against a fitness threshold $m$ (missions the unit must survive):

| Action | $r \ge m$ (fit) | $r < m$ (unfit) |
|---|---|---|
| Relaunch | $0$ | $C_{\text{fail}}$ — dominant term |
| Human review | $c_{\text{insp}}$ | $c_{\text{insp}}$ |
| Scrap | $c_{\text{unit}}$ | $0$ |

3. Choose $a^{\star} = \arg\min_a \mathbb{E}_{r \sim p(r \mid x)}\,[\,C(a, r)\,]$.

**What this buys.** The three-way verdict falls out of the cost structure instead of being hand-thresholded; "human review" is selected automatically exactly where the posterior is too wide for either committal action to be cheap. The cost ratios become explicit, auditable numbers an engineer can argue with — not hyperparameters. And it degrades gracefully: hard violations from the deterministic checks can enter as a prior that forces $P(r < m) \to 1$ without changing the machinery.

The POMDP stays in the document as future work, conditional on a simulator existing.

## 9. Model ladder

| Tier | Models | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Random forest, XGBoost | Reference numbers; feature importance is readable |
| Uncertainty | Quantile regression forest | Produces $p(r \mid x)$ — the input the decision layer actually needs |
| Sequence | LSTM, TCN, 1D-CNN over sliding windows | Tests whether temporal structure beats per-row features |

Compared on the asymmetric score, on RMSE, and — the point of the exercise — on **calibration of the predicted interval**, since a decision rule built on a badly calibrated posterior is worse than one built on no posterior at all.

## 10. Open questions for discussion

1. **Threshold $m$** — how many missions must a cleared unit be certified for? Sets where the decision boundary sits.
2. **Cost ratios** — is there a defensible figure for $C_{\text{fail}} : c_{\text{unit}} : c_{\text{insp}}$, or should the proposal present verdicts as a function of the ratio?
3. **Human-review budget** — an unconstrained cost matrix may route more units to inspection than an engineering team can absorb. Worth adding a capacity constraint?
4. **Is the one-shot framing right**, or does feeding a verdict forward across missions (registry → prior on the next flight) make the sequential formulation worth keeping?
